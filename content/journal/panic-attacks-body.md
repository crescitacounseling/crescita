Most people remember their first panic attack in detail. You're in the checkout line on a Saturday, or merging onto I-25 at rush hour, and out of nowhere your heart is pounding, your hands are tingling, and you can't get a full breath. A lot of people end up in the ER that first time, sure it's their heart.

Here's what those panic attack symptoms actually are: your body's emergency response, firing hard when there's no emergency. A pounding heart, shortness of breath, chest tightness, dizziness, shaking, sweating, nausea, a strange sense that things aren't real, and a powerful fear that you're dying or losing your mind. It's terrifying. It's also predictable, and once you can see the sequence, it gets a lot less mysterious.

One honest caveat first. Chest pain, fainting, or any symptom that's new or different from your usual should be checked by a doctor, especially the first time. Heart and thyroid problems can look a lot like panic, and only a medical exam can tell them apart. Getting checked is never overreacting.

## How panic attack symptoms unfold, step by step

It really does happen in order, usually within seconds:

1. **Something trips the alarm.** A thought, a crowded room, a skipped heartbeat, sometimes nothing you can name. Your brain's threat detector reacts before the thinking part of your brain has a chance to weigh in.
2. **Adrenaline hits.** Your adrenal glands dump adrenaline into your bloodstream. This is the jolt, the hot flush up the back of your neck.
3. **Your heart speeds up and pounds.** It's pushing blood to your big muscles so you can run. You might feel it in your throat or ears.
4. **Your breathing gets fast and shallow.** Breathing that fast lowers the carbon dioxide in your blood, which causes tingling in your fingers and lips, lightheadedness, and (strangely) the feeling that you can't get enough air.
5. **Blood moves away from your gut and skin.** Hence the nausea, the cold hands, the sudden urgent need for a bathroom.
6. **Your muscles brace.** The muscles around your ribs tighten, which is a big part of that chest pressure. Your jaw clenches. Your legs shake.
7. **The world looks wrong.** Lights seem too bright, sounds too far away, your own hands not quite yours. For many people this is the scariest part.
8. **Your mind reaches for an explanation.** "I'm having a heart attack." "I'm going crazy." "I'm going to pass out in front of everyone." That thought adds fear, and fear sends you right back to step 2.
9. **It peaks, then it comes down.** Adrenaline doesn't last forever. Most panic attacks peak within about ten minutes, and your body's calming system slowly takes over. Afterward you feel wrung out, shaky, sometimes wiped out for the rest of the day.

Every one of those steps is your body doing exactly what it was built to do. It's just doing it at the wrong time.

## Why one attack turns into a pattern

After the first one, most people start watching their bodies like a hawk. A flutter in the chest on the drive to work, a little breathlessness on the stairs, and the alarm goes off again, because now the body itself feels like the danger.

Then the world starts shrinking. You stop driving on the highway and take Academy the whole way. You skip Costco on weekends. You sit on the aisle at church, near the door, every single week. Each avoidance brings relief, and each one teaches your brain that the place really was dangerous.

The cruel loop is that panic doesn't need the original trigger anymore. The fear of having another attack is enough to start one. People tell me they feel fine all day and then get anxious about the drive home because that's where the last one happened, and that worry alone is enough to tip their body into step 1.

Panic often shows up after a long stretch of stress, too. If yours started during a hard season, it's worth reading about [how anxiety and burnout overlap](/journal/anxiety-or-burnout).

## Getting your life back from it

Panic is one of the most treatable things we see. That's worth saying plainly, because people who have it often feel like it's permanent.

In [anxiety therapy](/services/anxiety-therapy), we work on two fronts. One is practical: learning the sequence above until it's familiar, practicing ways to ride a wave out, and slowly returning to the places you've been avoiding, at a pace you choose. Sometimes that even means bringing on the sensations on purpose, like jogging in place to get your heart rate up, so your body can learn that a fast heartbeat on its own isn't a sign of disaster. The other goes deeper. Why did your alarm get so sensitive? For some people it's years of running on empty. For others, panic is tied to earlier experiences that taught the body the world isn't safe, and [EMDR](/services/emdr-therapy) can help those memories stop setting off the alarm. Sessions are available in our Colorado Springs office or online anywhere in Colorado, which can help if leaving the house is part of what panic has taken.

## If one is starting right now

Make your exhale longer than your inhale: in for about four counts, out slowly for six or eight, like you're cooling soup. Press your feet into the floor and notice the pressure. Then tell yourself, out loud if you can, "This is panic. It peaks and it passes." Don't fight the wave. Let it crest. It will come down.
