If you've had a panic attack, you probably remember the first one. A sudden wave of fear, a pounding heart, trouble breathing, tingling hands, the sense that something terrible is about to happen. Many people end up in an emergency room convinced it's a heart attack.

Panic attacks are frightening. They're also very understandable once you know what's going on.

## Your alarm system, firing at the wrong time

Your body has a built-in alarm designed to keep you alive. When it senses danger, it floods you with adrenaline and gets you ready to fight or run:

- Your heart speeds up to move blood to your muscles
- Your breathing gets faster and shallower
- Blood moves away from your hands and stomach, causing tingling or nausea
- Your senses sharpen, which can make things feel unreal or too bright

All of that is useful if a car is swerving toward you. In a panic attack, the alarm fires when there's no immediate danger. Your body is doing exactly what it's built to do, just at the wrong moment.

## Why they keep happening

After a first attack, many people start watching their bodies closely. A skipped heartbeat or a short breath gets read as a warning sign, which triggers more fear, which triggers the alarm again. The fear of panic becomes its own fuel.

People also start avoiding places where an attack happened: the grocery store, the highway, a crowded event. Avoidance brings short-term relief and long-term shrinking.

> A panic attack feels dangerous, but it isn't. It usually peaks within ten minutes and then passes on its own.

## What helps in the moment

- **Slow your exhale.** Breathe in for about four counts and out for six or eight. A longer exhale tells your nervous system to settle.
- **Name it.** "This is panic. It's uncomfortable. It will pass." Naming it takes away some of the mystery.
- **Ground through your senses.** Feel your feet on the floor, hold something cold, name five things you can see.
- **Don't fight it.** Paradoxically, letting the wave come and go tends to shorten it.

Always check with a doctor if you're unsure whether symptoms are medical, especially the first time.

## What helps long term

Coping tools matter, and they're only part of the picture. Therapy can help you understand why your alarm became so sensitive in the first place. For some people, panic grows out of years of stress or burnout. For others, it's linked to earlier experiences that taught the body the world isn't safe.
