Every child goes through hard stretches. Moodiness, clinginess, a bad semester, a falling-out with a friend. Most of it passes on its own.

But sometimes a phase doesn't pass. Or it gets bigger. Parents often tell us they waited because they weren't sure it was "bad enough." There's no threshold you have to meet. Still, a few signs suggest it might be worth talking to someone.

## Changes that last

The key word is change. Watch for shifts from your child's usual self that last more than a few weeks:

- Sleeping much more or much less
- Eating noticeably more or less
- Pulling away from friends or activities they used to enjoy
- Grades dropping without a clear reason
- More irritability, tears, or meltdowns than usual

## Worry that gets in the way

Some worry is normal. It becomes a concern when it starts running the show: refusing to go to school, avoiding sleepovers or activities, needing constant reassurance, or having frequent stomachaches and headaches with no medical cause.

## Big reactions to small things

When a child seems to go from zero to sixty over minor frustrations, it can mean they're carrying more than they can say. Kids often show stress through behavior long before they can put it into words.

## After something hard happened

Divorce, a death, a move, a scary event, a parent deploying. Even resilient kids can benefit from a space to process big changes. You don't need to wait for problems to show up.

## When your gut says something is off

Parents notice things. If you have a nagging sense that your child is struggling, that's worth trusting, even if you can't point to a specific reason.

> If your child talks about wanting to die or hurt themselves, act right away. Call or text 988, or call 911 in an emergency.

## What child therapy actually looks like

For younger kids, therapy often looks like play. Drawing, games, building things. Play is how kids work through feelings they don't have words for yet. Teens usually talk, though sometimes it takes a few sessions before they want to.

Parents aren't left on the outside. We share what's appropriate, give you context, and help you respond at home in ways that support the work. Your child still gets a space that's theirs.

## Starting the conversation

Try keeping it simple and non-shaming: "Lots of people talk to someone when things feel hard. I found someone who's really good at listening. Want to try it?"
