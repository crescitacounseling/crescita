Most kids hit rough patches. A seven-year-old gets clingy after a new baby comes home. A twelve-year-old has a miserable month in a new friend group. A fifteen-year-old sleeps until noon every Saturday and answers every question with "fine." A lot of that passes on its own, and you don't need a professional for every hard week.

So how do you tell the difference? The clearest signs your child needs therapy have less to do with any single behavior and more to do with change and time. Something shifts away from who your kid usually is, it sticks around longer than a few weeks, and it starts costing them things they care about.

Parents tell us all the time that they waited because they weren't sure it was "bad enough." There's no bar to clear. If you're reading this, you've probably already noticed something.

## Signs your child needs therapy

None of these on its own means something is wrong. Look for clusters, and for anything that's getting bigger instead of smaller.

### At home

- Meltdowns over small things (the wrong cup, a sock seam that feels weird) that are new, or happening a lot more than they used to
- Sleeping much more or much less, or suddenly needing to sleep in your room again
- Pulling away from friends, siblings, or activities they used to love
- Needing constant reassurance: the same worried question over and over, checking the locks, checking that you're coming back
- Going back to younger behaviors like bedwetting, baby talk, or not wanting to be alone in a room
- A teen who goes from private (normal) to completely sealed off (worth a closer look)

### At school

- Stomachaches or headaches on school mornings that the pediatrician can't explain
- Calls from the nurse's office, or begging to stay home every Monday
- Grades sliding for no obvious reason, especially in a kid who used to care
- A teacher mentioning that your child seems checked out, tearful, or is getting into fights at recess
- Losing their usual friend group and not finding a new one

### After something big

Divorce, a death in the family, a move, a parent deploying from Fort Carson, a car accident, a scary diagnosis for someone they love. Kids don't need to be falling apart to benefit from talking it through. Sometimes the kid who seems totally fine after a big loss is the one carrying the most.

> If your child talks about wanting to die, wishing they weren't here, or hurting themselves, take it seriously and act today. Call or text 988 (the Suicide & Crisis Lifeline), or call 911 if they're in immediate danger.

## That nagging feeling counts

Parents are often the first to see it and the last to trust it. You know how your kid laughs, what their face does when they're nervous, how long a sulk usually lasts. When that pattern breaks, you register it before you can explain it.

You don't need a checklist to call. A lot of our first conversations start with "I can't put my finger on it, but she's just not herself." That's plenty. The free 20-minute consult is for parents too, and if it sounds like a phase that will pass, we'll say so.

## What the first month with a child therapist looks like

The first appointment is usually mostly you. We want the history: when things changed, what you've tried, what home and school look like right now. Then your child comes in.

For younger kids, sessions often look like play. Drawing, a card game, building something with their hands while they talk sideways about what's bothering them. Play is how a seven-year-old works through things they don't have words for yet, which is why [Alissa Brown, who works with kids and teens on our team](/team/alissa-brown), uses play-based approaches with younger clients.

Teens mostly talk. It can take a few sessions before they want to. Some sit through the first twenty minutes saying almost nothing, and that's fine. A teenager who figures out the adult in the room isn't grading them usually starts talking eventually.

## What parents usually ask

### What age can my child start?

We see kids from age 7 up through the teen years. For children younger than that, most of the work goes through the parents, and we're glad to suggest where to look.

### Will the therapist tell me what my child says?

Not word for word, and that's on purpose. Kids open up when they trust that the room belongs to them. What you will hear: the themes, how things are going, and what you can do at home. If there's ever a safety concern, you'll know right away. We go over all of this with you and your child together at the start, so nobody gets surprised later.

### How do I explain therapy to my kid?

Keep it short and keep the shame out of it. For a younger child: "You know how you have a doctor for your body? This is someone who helps with big feelings. You'll mostly talk and play." For a teen, be honest and give them a say: "Things have seemed heavy lately. I found someone who's easy to talk to. Try three sessions, and if you hate it, we'll figure out something else."

Don't frame it as a consequence for behavior. A kid who thinks therapy is a punishment will spend the first month proving it doesn't work.
