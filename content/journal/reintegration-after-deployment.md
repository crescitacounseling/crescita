The homecoming is the part everyone sees. Handmade signs, a gym or hangar full of families, a little kid sprinting across the floor in a shirt that says "My Daddy's Home" or "My Mom's Back." It's real, and it's one of the best days a family can have.

Then everybody drives home, and the next morning someone has to figure out who's taking the kids to school.

Reintegration after deployment is the part that gets less attention, and it's often harder than families expect. People plan for relief and find something more tangled. That's normal. It helps a lot to know ahead of time what tends to come up.

## Nobody stayed the same

While a service member is gone, the household doesn't pause. It reorganizes. The parent at home becomes the only decision-maker for six, nine, twelve months. They learn the furnace, the car registration, the bedtime routine for three kids at once. They build a rhythm that works because it had to.

The kids change too, sometimes visibly. A toddler who was crawling at the farewell is now running and talking. A ten-year-old started a new sport and has a coach they quote at dinner. A teenager got a driver's permit, a girlfriend, and a whole set of opinions.

Meanwhile the service member has been living in a completely different world, with its own pace, rules, and stress. Their body has been operating on high alert for a long time.

Then everyone sits down at the same table, more or less expecting to pick up where they left off. Nobody is quite the person who said goodbye.

> Reintegration isn't one moment at the hangar. It's a season, and it usually runs longer than anyone planned.

## Where reintegration after deployment gets bumpy

**Roles.** The spouse at home has been running everything and may be both relieved and reluctant to hand pieces back. The returning parent can feel like a guest in their own house, or try to take over too fast and step on systems that were working fine. Neither of you is wrong. The friction usually lives in the gap between two reasonable expectations.

One thing that helps: in the first week or two, sit down and literally write out who does what now. Bills, school emails, discipline, the dog's vet appointments. Then decide together which two or three things the returning parent picks up first, and which ones wait a month. Going slow on purpose feels strange to people who are used to getting things done. It also prevents a lot of resentment on both sides.

**Kids' reactions.** Young children may be shy, or even wary, of a parent they half remember. Some cling hard. School-age kids often test limits with the returning parent ("Mom lets me"). Teens might act like they don't care, which is rarely the whole story.

**The body coming down.** Plenty of service members feel numb, on edge, or hard to reach for a while. Sleep is off. Crowds, traffic on Powers Boulevard, or a loud restaurant can be a lot. Irritability shows up out of nowhere.

**The couple.** Two people who wrote "I miss you" for months can find themselves arguing about the dishwasher within a week. That's common. Short, regular check-ins without the kids help more than one big conversation.

Some of this settles on its own. Watch for things that hold on past a month or two: nightmares or flashbacks, strong reactions to reminders, drinking more, a child whose struggles aren't easing, or arguments becoming the default. Military OneSource and the counseling programs on Fort Carson and the other installations are good places to start. ## A first month that's going okay

It probably doesn't look like a movie. It looks more like this.

By week two, the returning parent has taken back two things, maybe Tuesday and Thursday school pickups and Saturday pancakes, and left the rest alone for now. The spouse who held the house together has noticed they can finish a cup of coffee while it's still warm.

The four-year-old still runs to the other parent when she falls down. But last night she asked the returning parent to read the bedtime book, and then asked for it again.

There's been at least one real argument. It ended with someone saying "I didn't know you'd been doing it that way," and the dishwasher got loaded the new way.

Costco on a Saturday is still too much, so the service member waits in the car, and nobody makes it a thing. Sleep is better than week one, not great.

The teenager hasn't said much. But he showed up at the kitchen counter at 10 p.m. to show his dad a video of his driving lesson, and stayed twenty minutes.
