The homecoming is the part everyone sees: the signs, the hugs, the kids running across the hangar. It's a real and beautiful moment.

What comes after gets less attention. Families often expect relief and find something more complicated. That's normal, and it helps to know what to expect.

## Everyone changed

While a service member is deployed, the family at home keeps going. The spouse at home becomes the sole decision-maker. Kids grow, start new things, and form new routines. The household rearranges itself around the absence.

Meanwhile, the service member has lived in a completely different world with its own rhythms, demands, and stresses.

Then everyone comes back together expecting to pick up where they left off. But nobody is quite the same person who said goodbye.

## Common bumps in reintegration

**Roles and routines.** Who handles the bills, the bedtime routine, the discipline? The parent at home has been running things. The returning parent may feel like a visitor in their own house.

**Kids' reactions.** Young children may be shy or even wary at first. Older kids might act out, test limits, or seem distant. Some cling. All of it is a normal way of adjusting.

**Emotional distance.** Service members may feel numb, on edge, or hard to reach. It can take time for the body to come down from a high-alert setting.

**Sleep and irritability.** Trouble sleeping, short tempers, and difficulty with crowds or noise are common in the first weeks and months.

> Reintegration isn't a single moment. It's a season, and it often takes longer than anyone planned.

## What helps

- **Go slow.** Give the returning parent time to ease back into roles rather than taking everything over at once.
- **Talk about the changes.** What's different at home now? What's working? What needs adjusting?
- **Keep kids' routines steady.** Familiar structure helps them feel secure while the family recalibrates.
- **Lower expectations for the first weeks.** You don't have to have the perfect reunion.
- **Make time for the couple.** Even short check-ins without the kids can help partners reconnect.

## When to get more support

Some adjustment is expected. It's worth reaching out if:

- Irritability, numbness, or sleep problems continue for more than a month or two
- There are nightmares, flashbacks, or strong reactions to reminders
- Drinking increases
- Arguments are becoming the norm
- A child's struggles aren't easing

Military OneSource and on-installation programs are good resources. Some families also want an off-post option for longer-term support or to keep things outside the chain of command.
