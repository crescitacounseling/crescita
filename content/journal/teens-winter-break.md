On paper, winter break should be the easiest stretch of the school year. No homework, no early alarms, two or three weeks to rest.

For a lot of teenagers it doesn't work out that way. They come off finals running on fumes. The structure that kept them going disappears overnight. Sleep schedules flip. Phones fill the gap. By New Year's, some kids feel worse than they did in November.

## Why the break can be hard

Teens often hold it together at school and fall apart at home. That's not manipulation. Home is where it's safe enough to stop performing.

Winter break also strips away the things that quietly regulate them: routines, friends in person, sports, even the bus ride. Without those, anxiety and low mood have more room to grow. Add holiday family tension and a constant scroll of other people's highlight reels, and it adds up.

## Signs to watch for

Some withdrawal and a lot of sleep are normal for teenagers. What's worth noticing is a change from their usual:

- Sleeping most of the day, or barely sleeping
- Pulling away from friends they usually see
- Irritability that seems bigger than the situation
- Losing interest in things they normally enjoy
- Talking about feeling hopeless, worthless, or like a burden
- Dread or stomachaches as school gets closer

If your teen talks about wanting to die or hurting themselves, take it seriously and get help right away. Call or text 988, or call 911 in an emergency.

## What helps

**Keep a loose rhythm.** Not a schedule, just a few anchors. A wake-up window, one meal together, getting outside once a day. Colorado's sunny winter days help more than you'd think.

**Connect without interrogating.** Teens often talk more in the car, while cooking, or late at night than in a sit-down conversation. Side by side works better than face to face.

**Get curious before you correct.** "You seem wiped out lately. What's been the hardest part?" opens more doors than "You need to get off your phone."

**Make room for them to do nothing.** Some rest really is rest. The goal isn't productivity. It's helping them come back to themselves before January.

> Your teen doesn't need you to fix it. They need to know you noticed and you're not going anywhere.

## When to get support

If the hard stretch outlasts the break, or your gut tells you something bigger is going on, it's worth talking to someone.
