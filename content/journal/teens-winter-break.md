It's Thursday, day four of break. Your sixteen-year-old came downstairs at 1:40 in the afternoon, ate cereal standing at the counter, and went back up. The blinds in their room are still closed. Around 2 a.m. you heard them laugh at something on their phone, and that's the last time they sounded happy. When you asked if they wanted to drive up to Manitou for lunch, they said "maybe later" in the voice that means no.

Last week they were pulling late nights for finals. You figured a few days of sleep would fix it.

It hasn't. This is one of the less talked-about forms of teen stress: the break that's supposed to restore them sometimes knocks them flat first.

## Why the crash comes after finals

Plenty of teenagers run on adrenaline from Thanksgiving to the last exam. Papers, tests, the winter concert, practices before the holiday tournament. The pressure is miserable, and it also keeps them upright. When it stops, the body finally registers how tired it is, and mood often drops right along with it.

Then the scaffolding goes. No 6:45 alarm. No lunch table. No coach expecting them at 3:30. Those things look like obligations from the outside, but for a lot of kids they're also what keeps anxiety and low mood in check. Without them, the days lose their shape. Bedtime slides an hour later each night until noon feels normal. The phone fills every gap, and the feed is full of other kids in Breckenridge, or in Cabo, or at a party they weren't invited to.

Add a house full of relatives, a parent deployed over the holidays, or the first Christmas split between two homes, and the "relaxing" break can turn into the hardest two weeks of the semester. (If the family part is weighing on you too, we wrote about [why the holidays stir up old family stuff](/journal/holidays-family-stuff).)

One more thing. Teens often hold it together at school and fall apart at home. That's exhausting to be on the receiving end of. It also means they feel safe enough with you to stop performing.

## Teen stress worth a closer look

Sleeping until eleven is normal. Wanting a day or two alone is normal. Being annoyed with a little brother is extremely normal.

Pay closer attention when something lasts more than a week or looks different from your teen's usual self:

- Sleeping most of the day and still seeming exhausted, or barely sleeping at all
- Turning down plans with the friends they usually can't get enough of
- Snapping in a way that's far bigger than the moment
- Dropping things they love, like not touching the guitar or skipping the ski day they asked for back in October
- Saying "what's the point," or calling themselves a burden
- Dread that builds as January gets closer, showing up as stomachaches or Sunday night fights

If your teen talks about wanting to die or hurting themselves, don't wait to see if it passes. Call or text 988, the Suicide & Crisis Lifeline, or call 911 if they're in immediate danger.

## Small moves for the rest of break

Skip the full schedule. It usually backfires. Two or three anchors are plenty: a wake-up window (10:30 is fine, 2 p.m. is not), one meal where people actually sit down together, and some daylight. Colorado hands out a lot of sunny winter afternoons, and a twenty-minute loop through Monument Valley Park does more for a flat mood than most lectures.

Talk side by side. Teenagers say more in the car, while you're both chopping onions, or at 11 p.m. when you're half asleep than they ever will across a table with your full attention on them. Steady eye contact can feel like an interrogation.

Ask before you correct. "You've seemed really wiped out since finals. What's been the worst part?" will get you further than "You need to get off your phone." You can still set limits on screens. Just ask first.

Leave room for real rest, too. A teen who spends one whole day watching movies with the dog isn't failing at anything. Nobody needs a productive winter break.

And if you don't know how to open the door, try something like the words below. Say it once, casually, in the car or the kitchen. Then go back to what you were doing. Walking away gently matters as much as what you say.

> "Hey. You're not in trouble. You've seemed kind of low since break started, and I just want you to know I see it. You don't have to talk about it right now. But if something's going on, I'm around, and I'd rather know than not know."
