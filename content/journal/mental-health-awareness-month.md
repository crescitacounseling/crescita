May is Mental Health Awareness Month, and every year my feed fills up with the same things. Morning routines. Gratitude journals. Ten steps to a better you, usually posted by someone with a very clean kitchen.

Some of it helps. A lot of it lands as one more thing you're failing at, especially if you're already having a hard time. So I want to say something simpler.

Knowing what helps and doing it are two very different skills. Nobody is exempt from that, therapists included.

## What Mental Health Awareness Month leaves out

Most of what actually helps is boring. It doesn't photograph well.

It's getting up at the same time most days, even when you slept badly. It's eating something with protein before 2 p.m. It's the walk around the block after dinner, or a slow loop at Garden of the Gods on a Saturday morning while the light is still soft on the rocks. It's texting your sister back instead of letting it sit for a week.

None of those will fix depression or trauma. They're not supposed to. They keep the floor from dropping out while you figure out what else you need.

The other thing that gets skipped is how much the stories in our heads keep people stuck. "Other people have it worse." "I should be over this by now." "I'm too much." I hear versions of these every week from people who waited years to call. A useful test: would you say that sentence to a friend sitting across from you? If not, it probably isn't true about you either.

## The signs I'd pay attention to

Small steps are real, and they aren't a substitute for care when you need it. It may be time to talk to someone if:

- You've felt low, anxious, or numb for more than a few weeks
- Sleep, work, or your relationships are taking the hit
- You're leaning harder on alcohol, food, or your phone to get through the evening
- Something old keeps showing up in how you react now

If you're having thoughts of suicide, call or text 988, the Suicide & Crisis Lifeline, any time. If you or someone else is in immediate danger, call 911.

## If you've been waiting for it to get bad enough

You don't need a crisis to start therapy. You don't need the right words either. Plenty of people open a first session with "I'm not totally sure why I'm here," and that's a fine place to begin.

If a past experience with a therapist didn't help, that's worth taking seriously, and it doesn't mean therapy can't work for you. Fit matters a lot.
