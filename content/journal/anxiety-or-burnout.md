You're exhausted but you can't sleep. Small tasks feel enormous. You snap at people you like. Somewhere in the back of your mind a voice keeps asking whether something is wrong with you.

A lot of people land in our office not sure what to call this. Is it anxiety? Burnout? Both? The answer matters less than you'd think, but understanding the difference can make the next step a lot clearer.

## What burnout usually looks like

Burnout tends to come from a specific source. A job, caregiving, a stretch of parenting with no breaks. It builds over months of giving more than you get back.

The hallmark is depletion. You feel flat, cynical, or detached from things that used to matter. You might still be functioning, but it takes everything you've got. When you finally get a real break (a week away, a long weekend with nothing on the calendar) you start to come back to yourself.

## What anxiety usually looks like

Anxiety is less about running out and more about running hot. Your mind scans for what could go wrong. Your body stays braced, even on a quiet Sunday. The worry follows you on vacation.

Anxiety also tends to attach itself to new things. Fix one worry and another steps into its place. That's a clue that the problem isn't really the thing you're worried about.

## Where they overlap

Here's the tricky part: they feed each other. Anxious people often over-function, which leads to burnout. Burnout lowers your reserves, which makes anxiety louder.

Some signs show up in both:

- Trouble sleeping, or sleeping and waking up tired
- Irritability and a short fuse
- Difficulty concentrating
- Tight shoulders, headaches, stomach trouble
- Pulling away from friends

> A useful question: if the thing draining you disappeared tomorrow, would you feel relief, or would your mind go looking for the next thing to worry about?

If it's mostly relief, burnout is probably the bigger piece. If your mind would find something new, anxiety is likely doing more of the work.

## What helps

For burnout, the fix is partly practical. Something has to give: hours, expectations, the number of people depending on you. Rest helps, but only if you're able to actually rest instead of catching up on everything else.

For anxiety, rest alone rarely does it. It helps to understand what the anxiety is protecting you from. For many people it started as a smart strategy. Staying alert kept you safe in a chaotic home. Being perfect kept criticism away. The strategy just never got the message that things changed.

In therapy we look at both layers: what needs to change in your life right now, and what your nervous system learned a long time ago. Sometimes that includes [EMDR](/services/emdr-therapy) for older experiences that keep the alarm stuck on.
