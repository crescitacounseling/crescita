By the third week of June, a lot of parents notice something odd. There's no homework, nobody has to be anywhere at 7:40, and their kid seems more anxious than during finals. Clingier. Stuck on what-ifs. Falling apart when plans change.

It makes sense once you see what school was quietly doing. For anxious kids, summer takes away the predictable shape of the day: the same bell, the same faces, a teacher who tells them what comes next. Anxiety feeds on not knowing. Remove the structure and a worried brain starts filling in the blanks, usually with the worst version.

## A loose shape for an anxious kid's summer day

You don't need a color-coded schedule. Too much structure brings its own trouble, and anxious kids need some boredom to practice settling themselves. A few fixed points are enough:

- Waking up at about the same time, give or take half an hour
- Meals at roughly the same times
- One outdoor block, ideally in the morning before the afternoon thunderstorms build
- The same wind-down routine at night, even if bedtime is later than in May

Movement does a lot of the work. A morning at a splash pad, a bike ride on the Santa Fe Trail, a short loop at Red Rock Canyon Open Space. Nervous energy has to go somewhere.

Screens are the hard one. They aren't the enemy, and a rainy afternoon of cartoons is fine. The trouble is the long, shapeless day that turns into four hours of scrolling, which tends to leave anxious kids more wound up, not less. A couple of screen-free windows that happen at the same time every day (say, before lunch and after dinner) cause fewer fights than limits you invent on the spot.

Keep one standing social thing with the same kids each week, so friendships don't go dormant and August doesn't bring a fresh wave of dread about who they'll know.

If worry is starting to run the day (turning down every invitation, needing reassurance every few minutes, stomachaches with no medical cause), it may be more than a summer wobble. Our post on [signs your child needs therapy](/journal/signs-child-needs-therapy) can help you sort it out. Summer is actually a good time to start [child and teen therapy](/services/child-and-teen-therapy), with no homework or school pressure in the mix.

## Sunday night, the fridge, and a marker

Here's the one tool I'd pick if you only try one thing this summer.

Every Sunday evening, sit down with your child and a paper calendar for the week. Stick it on the fridge where they'll walk past it twenty times a day. Let them write in the plans themselves: swim lessons Tuesday, Grandma Thursday, library on Friday. Younger kids can draw a picture instead of writing.

Leave the empty days empty and label them "free day." That's information too.

When something is uncertain, don't leave it off. Write it with a question mark and a plan: "Pool party? We'll decide Friday morning." Anxious kids handle a known unknown much better than a surprise.

Then, when the fifth "what are we doing tomorrow" comes, skip the answer and point at the fridge. Within a couple of weeks, most kids start checking it on their own.
