The school supplies are bought, the forms are signed, and your child has a stomachache every night. Or they can't fall asleep. Or they've asked the same question about their new teacher fourteen times.

Back-to-school anxiety is very common. A little nervousness before something new is normal and even healthy. Here's how to help, and how to tell when it's more than nerves.

## Why the start of school is stressful

The first weeks bring a lot at once: new teachers, new routines, new classmates, academic pressure, and for many kids, social worries about where they fit. Kids who are changing schools, starting middle or high school, or arriving with a military move often feel it most.

## How it shows up

Kids don't always say "I'm anxious." It often looks like:

- Stomachaches, headaches, or nausea, especially on school mornings
- Trouble falling asleep or waking at night
- Irritability, tears, or meltdowns
- Clinginess or not wanting to separate
- Lots of what-if questions
- Saying they don't want to go

## What helps

**Practice the routine early.** Shift bedtimes back a week or two before school starts. Do a test run of the morning: alarm, breakfast, getting out the door.

**Visit the school.** Walking the halls, finding the classroom, or meeting the teacher ahead of time takes a lot of mystery out of the first day.

**Validate, then build confidence.** "It makes sense to feel nervous about something new. You've done hard new things before." Avoid "There's nothing to worry about," which can make kids feel unheard.

**Keep goodbyes short and warm.** Long goodbyes tend to increase anxiety. A predictable ritual (a hug, a phrase, a high five) helps.

**Plan something to look forward to.** A favorite snack after school or a walk together gives the day a soft landing.

> Your calm is contagious. Kids borrow steadiness from the adults around them.

## When it's more than nerves

Most back-to-school anxiety fades within a few weeks as routines settle. It may be time to get support if:

- Anxiety is getting stronger instead of easing
- Your child is refusing school or missing a lot of days
- Physical complaints continue with no medical cause
- Panic attacks happen
- Worry is spilling into sleep, friendships, and activities

Talk to the school counselor, and consider [child and teen therapy](/services/child-and-teen-therapy). Kids can learn tools to handle anxiety, and parents can learn how to support them without accidentally feeding the worry.
