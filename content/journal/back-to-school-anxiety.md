It usually starts about when the school supply lists go up. Your kid who was fine all July suddenly has a stomachache at bedtime. Or can't fall asleep. Or has asked you four times today whether the new teacher will be nice, and you've answered four times, and it hasn't helped.

That's back to school anxiety, and it's one of the most common things parents ask us about in August. Some nervousness before something new is normal and even useful, since it's the brain getting ready. For the next two weeks, aim to give the nerves less to feed on. You won't make them disappear, and you don't need to.

## How back to school anxiety shows up

Younger children rarely say "I'm anxious." They say their tummy hurts. They cling at drop-off for swim lessons they used to love. They melt down over the wrong socks.

Teens are more likely to go quiet, snap at you, or stay up scrolling until 1 a.m. because lying in the dark with their thoughts is worse.

The kids who tend to feel it most are starting a new school, moving up to middle or high school, or arriving in Colorado Springs mid-move with no idea where anything is. If that last one is your family, our post on [helping military kids through a PCS move](/journal/military-kids-pcs-move) goes deeper.

## The two weeks before the first day

Think of it as a slow ramp. You don't have to hit every step, and the order matters more than the exact day.

1. **Two weeks out: start moving bedtime.** Fifteen minutes earlier every couple of nights. If your kid has been going to sleep at 10:30 all summer, a 6:45 alarm on day one is going to hurt, and tired kids worry more.
2. **Twelve days out: find out what the worry actually is.** Pick a calm moment (the car is good) and ask which part of starting school feels hardest. Then just listen. You'll often hear something smaller and more fixable than you expected, like where to sit at lunch or whether the bus driver will know their stop.
3. **Ten days out: solve one small thing together.** Practice a combination lock at the kitchen table. Look up the lunch menu. Walk to the bus stop.
4. **About a week out: see the building.** Many schools in District 11, Academy District 20, and the surrounding districts hold an open house or back-to-school night. Go. If you can't, drive over and walk the outside. Finding the exact door they'll use takes a lot of mystery out of the first morning.
5. **Six days out: do a full test run.** Alarm, breakfast, clothes, backpack, out the door at the real time. Then go do something fun so the morning ends well.
6. **Four days out: pick a goodbye ritual.** A specific hug, a phrase, a silly handshake. Keep it short and the same every time. Long, teary goodbyes at the classroom door usually make it harder on both of you.
7. **Two days out: plan the soft landing.** Something small to look forward to after the first day: a popsicle on the porch, a walk with the dog, their favorite dinner.
8. **The night before: keep it boring.** Clothes laid out, bag packed, normal bedtime. Skip the big pep talk.

Along the way, try validating before you reassure. "It makes sense to be nervous. New things are hard, and you've done hard new things before" lands better than "There's nothing to worry about," which tells a kid the feeling is wrong without making it any smaller.

Your own steadiness counts, too. Kids borrow calm from the adults around them. If you're nervous about the new school (plenty of parents are), it's fine to say so in an even voice and let them see what you do with it.

## If the dread is still there in September

Most back to school anxiety eases within the first few weeks, once the routine is familiar and the unknowns have faces and names. Look more closely if it's heading the other way: your child is refusing to go or missing a lot of days, the stomachaches and headaches keep coming with no medical cause, there are panic attacks, or the worry is starting to take over sleep, friendships, and activities they used to enjoy. At that point a good routine usually isn't enough by itself, and both the school counselor and a [child and teen therapist](/services/child-and-teen-therapy) can help. Kids can learn practical ways to handle anxious thoughts and body sensations, and parents can learn how to support them without accidentally feeding the worry, which is very easy to do out of love.
