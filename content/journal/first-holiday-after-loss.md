The first holiday season after someone dies has a way of ambushing people. You might be doing all right in October. Then "Have Yourself a Merry Little Christmas" comes on in the cereal aisle at Safeway, or your sister texts to ask who's hosting this year, and the floor drops out.

Grief during the holidays is heavier for reasons that make sense. The season runs on traditions, and traditions are made of people. Their chair at the table. Their handwriting on the gift tags. The way they burned the first batch of rolls every year and blamed the oven. When the calendar hands you all of that in the same six weeks, the absence gets loud.

There's no way to make this season easy. There are ways to make it a little more bearable.

## The pressure to keep things normal

Someone will probably say it. Keep everything the same for the kids. Do it because Dad would have wanted it. Sometimes that's true, and the familiar routine really is a comfort. Sometimes it's just exhausting, and you end up performing a holiday you don't feel.

You get to decide which one this is for you.

You're allowed to have a strange, quiet, sad holiday this year. You're also allowed to laugh at dinner, enjoy the pie, and have a genuinely good hour. Neither one is a betrayal. Grief and joy can sit at the same table, and in the first year after a loss they usually do, sometimes in the same five minutes.

It helps to talk with your family ahead of time about which traditions to keep, which to change, and which to skip. Some families keep everything exactly as it was. Some rent a cabin in Estes Park and leave the house behind for a few days. What matters is choosing on purpose, before you're standing at the table blindsided.

If your family already had its own tensions, a loss can stir those up too. This post on [why the holidays stir up old family stuff](/journal/holidays-family-stuff) might be useful alongside this one.

## Grief during the holidays: small ways to keep them close

Some people find comfort in giving the person a place in the day instead of working around the hole. Small is fine. Small is often better.

- Light a candle at dinner and leave it burning through dessert
- Make their recipe, even if it doesn't come out right
- Hang the ornament they always insisted go front and center
- Go around the table with one story each, funny ones included
- Write them a card and tuck it in their stocking
- Drive past their old house, or walk the Palmer Park loop where they used to take the dog
- Give to something they cared about, in their name
- Leave their chair empty, or invite someone to fill it on purpose

Pick one. Skip all of them if none feel right this year. Kids often do better with a job, like lighting the candle or choosing whose story goes first.

## Planning for the heaviest days

Some days will be harder than others: the holiday itself, their birthday, the anniversary of the death. Grief doesn't follow the calendar, but the calendar is still useful. A hard day you can see coming is a day you can plan for.

For each one, ask yourself who you want to be with, what might help even a little, and what you can take off your plate.

> "Let me know if you need anything" almost never turns into anything. Tell people exactly what would help.

People usually want to help and have no idea how. So give them something specific. Dinner on the 23rd. A ride to the airport. Someone to come over on the afternoon of the anniversary and watch a bad movie with you.

Keep an eye, too, on grief that gets stuck. If the death was sudden or traumatic, you may find yourself replaying the worst moments instead of remembering the person. If the relationship was complicated, guilt or anger can tangle everything up. If you're months in and still can't function, or the memories keep pushing in, that's a good time to talk with someone. If all you manage this year is getting from the morning of the 25th to bedtime, that is enough. Eat something warm. Let someone else do the dishes. Go to bed at eight if you want to. Next year will be its own kind of hard, and you can face it when it comes. For now, it's one day, and then the next one.
