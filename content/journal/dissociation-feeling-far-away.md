Some people describe it as watching their life through a window. Others say they lose chunks of an afternoon and can't say where it went, or that they drove all the way down Academy and don't remember a single light. One person might feel like the world went flat and fake, like a movie set. Another just feels nothing at all, right in the middle of something that should matter.

That experience has a name: dissociation. It's far more common than most people realize, and once you understand where it comes from, it usually makes a lot of sense.

## What dissociation actually is

Dissociation is a disconnection between things that normally travel together: your thoughts, your feelings, your body, your memories, and your sense of who you are.

Everyone does it a little. You zone out on a familiar drive. You get so absorbed in a book that someone has to say your name twice. That's ordinary, and nothing to worry about.

For some people it's stronger, more frequent, and harder to snap out of. It can feel like:

- Going numb or blank during moments that should feel big, like a wedding or a hard conversation
- Watching yourself from slightly outside or above your body
- The room looking foggy, far away, or not quite real
- Your own hands or voice seeming like they belong to someone else
- Gaps in memory, sometimes large stretches of childhood
- Losing time, or finding evidence of things you don't remember doing
- Different sides of you wanting very different things, almost like different people
- Drifting off whenever stress rises, then struggling to come back

Dissociation is a protective response. When something is too much to fight and impossible to escape, especially for a child, the mind finds another exit. It goes somewhere else. A kid in a frightening house can't move out. They can leave in the only way available, and that's a genuinely clever thing for a young nervous system to figure out.

> At some point, going far away was the smartest thing your system could do.

The trouble is that the response doesn't always switch off when the danger ends. Decades later it shows up in staff meetings, in arguments with a partner, during intimacy, at the pediatrician's office with your own kid, or on an ordinary Tuesday when you're tired. Life starts to feel muted, or slippery, or like it's happening to someone else. Many people with significant dissociation have spent years convinced something is deeply wrong with them. Usually something happened to them, and their system found a way through. That's a very different thing.

If dissociation started suddenly, or came on after a head injury, a new medication, or a medical change, please mention it to your doctor as well. That's worth ruling out.

## Coming back, slowly

With dissociation, slow is the fast way. Pushing straight into painful memories tends to make it worse, because the system that learned to leave will do exactly that. So the early part of therapy is about steadiness. We work on noticing the first signs that you're starting to drift (for a lot of people it's a buzzing in the hands, or sounds getting distant), finding what reliably brings you back, and getting to know the parts of you that take over in certain situations.

[Parts work](/services/parts-work-therapy) is especially useful here, because dissociation so often involves sides of you that feel quite separate from each other. When those parts feel understood rather than shoved aside, the whole system can relax a little. I wrote an introduction to [what parts work means](/journal/what-is-parts-work) if the idea is new to you. Later, once there's enough ground under you, EMDR can help process the memories that keep the alarm running. Dissociation and parts work are the center of my practice at Crescita, and I see adults in person in Colorado Springs and [online anywhere in Colorado](/services/online-therapy-colorado).

In the meantime, here's one thing to try the next time you notice yourself floating off. Press your feet into the floor hard enough to feel your heels, and look for something cold to hold, like a can from the fridge or the metal edge of your desk. Then say out loud, quietly, today's date and where you are: "It's Thursday, I'm in my kitchen in Colorado Springs." It sounds almost too simple, but naming the present gives the part of you that left a clear signal about where to come back to.
