Some people describe it as watching their life through a window. Others say they lose chunks of time, or feel like they're on autopilot, or that the world suddenly seems flat and unreal.

That experience has a name: dissociation. It's much more common than most people realize, and it usually makes a lot of sense once you understand where it comes from.

## What dissociation is

Dissociation is a disconnection between things that usually go together: your thoughts, feelings, body, memories, and sense of who you are.

Everyone dissociates a little. Driving home and not remembering the drive. Getting so lost in a book that you don't hear your name. That's normal.

For some people, it's stronger and happens more often:

- Feeling numb or empty, even during important moments
- Feeling detached from your body, as if you're observing yourself
- The world feeling foggy, dreamlike, or unreal
- Gaps in memory, sometimes big ones from childhood
- Feeling like different parts of you want very different things
- Zoning out when stress rises

## Why it happens

Dissociation is a protective response. When something is too much to fight or escape, especially for a child, the mind finds another way out. It goes somewhere else.

> Disconnection isn't a flaw. At some point, it was the smartest thing your system could do.

The trouble is that the response can stay on long after the danger has passed. Now it shows up in meetings, arguments, intimacy, or ordinary stress, and it can make life feel muted or hard to hold onto.

## How therapy helps

With dissociation, slow is the fast way. Pushing straight into painful memories can make it worse. Good therapy starts by building steadiness:

1. Noticing the early signs that you're starting to drift
2. Finding what helps bring you back, like naming things you can see or feeling your feet on the floor
3. Getting to know the parts of you that show up in different situations
4. Only then, gently working with the experiences underneath

[Parts work](/services/parts-work-therapy) is especially useful here, because dissociation often involves sides of you that feel quite separate. When those parts feel understood rather than pushed away, the whole system can relax. Later, [EMDR](/services/emdr-therapy) can help process the memories that keep the alarm running.

## You're not broken

Many people with dissociation have spent years thinking something is deeply wrong with them. Often, nothing is wrong. Something happened, and your system found a way through.
